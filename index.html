What is identity theft?

Identity theft happens when someone uses your personal or financial information without permission, often to open accounts, make purchases, obtain services, or impersonate you. It can involve a Social Security number, passport, driver’s license, bank details, medical information, passwords, or account recovery data.

🎣

Phishing and impersonation

Unexpected messages may pretend to be from a bank, delivery company, employer, or government agency. They often create urgency and ask for codes, passwords, or payment.

💳

Financial misuse

Stolen data can be used for unauthorized card purchases, new credit, account takeovers, tax fraud, or fraudulent payment requests.

📱

Account takeover

A criminal who gains access to email or a phone number may reset other passwords. Protect your primary email first because it is often the recovery key.

Safety rule: Never share a one-time verification code, password, PIN, or full account number with someone who contacted you unexpectedly. End the conversation and contact the organization using a trusted website or statement.

Warning signs to watch for

A single unfamiliar transaction can be a mistake, but multiple signals deserve prompt attention.

Money and credit

  • Unknown charges or withdrawals
  • Bills for accounts you did not open
  • Credit denials or score changes without explanation
  • Debt-collection calls about unfamiliar debts

Accounts and mail

  • Password-reset alerts you did not request
  • Missing mail or unexpected address changes
  • New login locations or devices
  • Messages confirming actions you did not take

Personal records

  • Medical bills or insurance claims you do not recognize
  • Tax notices for an unfiled return
  • Government benefits or employment records that are wrong
  • Calls asking you to “verify” sensitive details

If identity theft happens

Work from the most urgent risk to the least urgent. Keep a dated log of calls, case numbers, screenshots, letters, and actions taken.

Secure your accounts

Change the password for the affected account and your email. Use unique passwords and enable multifactor authentication. Sign out of unfamiliar sessions and update recovery information.

Contact the provider

Use the number on the back of your card or an official website—not a link in a suspicious message. Ask the provider to freeze, close, or investigate the account and send written confirmation.

Protect your credit

Contact the credit bureaus in your country and review your reports. In the United States, a credit freeze is free and restricts access to your file; a fraud alert asks businesses to verify identity.

Report and preserve evidence

Report scams to the appropriate government fraud-reporting service and local law enforcement when appropriate. Save originals, note dates, and avoid deleting suspicious messages until documented.

Urgent: If money is being transferred now, contact your bank or payment provider immediately. If you are in immediate physical danger, contact local emergency services.

Recovery checklist

Use this list to stay organized after discovering suspicious activity. Check off each action, record the date, and keep copies of every document you send.

Within the first hour

  • Stop responding to the suspected scammer.
  • Contact your bank, card issuer, or payment provider.
  • Change compromised passwords from a trusted device.
  • Save messages, receipts, URLs, and transaction details.

Within the first day

  • Review recent account activity and remove unknown devices.
  • Place a fraud alert or credit freeze where available.
  • Notify affected insurers, lenders, employers, or service providers.
  • File reports with the appropriate official agencies.

During follow-up

  • Keep an incident log with names, dates, and case numbers.
  • Dispute unauthorized transactions in writing.
  • Watch statements, credit reports, and account alerts regularly.
  • Follow up if written confirmation or corrections do not arrive.
Common scam pattern: A caller claims to be from a bank, police department, tax office, or technical-support team and demands immediate payment or a verification code. Hang up, then contact the organization through a number or website you found independently.

Everyday habits that help

Simple routines can reduce exposure and help you notice suspicious activity sooner.

Protect personal information

  • Share only the information a service genuinely needs.
  • Shred documents containing account or identification numbers.
  • Keep your mailbox and important records secure.

Think before clicking

  • Check the sender and destination before opening links.
  • Type important website addresses yourself or use trusted bookmarks.
  • Be cautious with urgent requests, attachments, and unusual payment methods.

Prepare for recovery

  • Keep official contact details for your bank and insurers.
  • Store multifactor recovery codes in a secure location.
  • Maintain an offline list of important accounts and providers.

Frequently asked questions

Should I pay a caller who threatens arrest or account closure?

No. Pressure, secrecy, unusual payment methods, and requests for gift cards or cryptocurrency are strong scam indicators. Verify independently using an official contact method.

What should I include in an incident log?

Record the date, time, organization, phone number or address, representative, case number, promises made, documents sent, and your next follow-up date.

Is a credit freeze the same as a fraud alert?

No. A freeze restricts access to a credit file until you lift it. A fraud alert tells businesses to take extra steps to verify an applicant. Availability and procedures depend on your country.

Can I prevent every kind of identity theft?

No security measure is perfect. Layered protection—unique passwords, multifactor authentication, careful verification, monitoring, and fast reporting—can reduce risk and limit harm.

Need official help? Start with your bank or card provider, your local consumer-protection authority, and the official government fraud-reporting service for your country. Do not use contact details supplied by a suspicious caller.
